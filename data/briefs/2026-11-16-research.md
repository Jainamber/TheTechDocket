## 1. Core Arguments of the Primary Source Essay
* **Training Debt & "License Laundering" [CONFIRMED_PRIMARY]:** 
  * Frontier AI models were trained by scraping massive open-source codebases (dating back to the launch of the GNU Project and Free Software Movement) without explicit permission or attribution.
  * Models also ingested community platforms like Stack Overflow and Stack Exchange, which host content under Creative Commons licenses (e.g., CC BY-SA) requiring attribution.
  * Turning this collective, attribution-locked knowledge into proprietary, closed-source commercial software is described by Jankiewicz as "license laundering".
* **The Legal/Copyrightability Argument [CONFIRMED_PRIMARY]:**
  * Purely AI-generated code lacks human authorship and is legally uncopyrightable.
  * Jankiewicz cites the "Monkey Selfie" dispute as the foundational U.S. precedent establishing that non-humans cannot hold copyrights—a principle subsequently adopted by the U.S. Copyright Office for AI outputs.
  * Wikimedia Commons already enforces this by requiring purely AI-generated images to be tagged as Public Domain.
* **The Global Jurisdictional Patchwork [CONFIRMED_PRIMARY]:**
  * Copyright laws are inconsistent globally; for example, the United Kingdom's Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 (CDPA) grants limited 50-year copyright protection to computer-generated works even without a human author.
  * To bypass this inconsistent patchwork and honor the open ecosystem that enabled AI, developers and companies should explicitly release AI-generated code under open-source licenses or dedicate it to the public domain using the Creative Commons Zero (CC0) license.
* **Prompt-Driven Development [CONFIRMED_PRIMARY]:**
  * While software development is often a hybrid of human and machine effort, developers increasingly rely on AI to write code without reviewing or modifying the output.
  * Code produced where human effort is limited strictly to writing conversational prompts should unconditionally be open-sourced.

## 2. Key Legal Precedents & Rulings (US Context)
* ***Thaler v. Perlmutter* (D.C. Cir. / US Supreme Court) [CONFIRMED_PRIMARY]:**
  * **March 18, 2025:** The U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit affirmed that human authorship is a bedrock requirement for copyright registration, upholding the U.S. Copyright Office's denial of Dr. Stephen Thaler's application to register an AI-generated artwork ("A Recent Entrance to Paradise") created by his "Creativity Machine".
  * **March 2, 2026:** The Supreme Court of the United States denied certiorari in *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, leaving the D.C. Circuit's human-authorship ruling intact as the final legal baseline.
* ***Doe v. GitHub, Inc.* (9th Circuit Court of Appeals) [CONFIRMED_PRIMARY]:**
  * **September 16, 2026:** A unanimous panel of the Ninth Circuit (Docket No. 24-7700) affirmed the dismissal of the programmers' Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) Section 1202(b) claims against GitHub, Microsoft, and OpenAI.
  * **The Ruling:** The court held that generative AI tools like Copilot and Codex do not "remove" or "alter" Copyright Management Information (CMI) when generating code because they probabilistically create *new* works rather than copying and stripping notices from stored files.
  * **Forfeited Input Theory:** The programmers' "input theory" (that the defendants stripped CMI from open-source code *before* feeding it into the training dataset) was ruled forfeited because their counsel failed to preserve it in the district court.
  * **Remaining Claims:** The ruling does not resolve ordinary copyright infringement claims (which the panel left open) or the two breach-of-contract claims still pending in the district court.

## 3. The India Angle: Legal & Regulatory Context
* **The "Raghav AI" Precedent [REPORTED_SECONDARY]:**
  * In November 2020, the Indian Copyright Office (ICO) initially granted registration to an AI-generated artwork titled "Suryast," listing the AI model RAGHAV (Robust Artificially Intelligent Graphics and Art Visualizer) as a co-author alongside human creator Ankit Sahni.
  * In December 2021, the ICO issued a withdrawal notice, declaring that an AI tool cannot be recognized as an "author" and that the human involvement was insufficient, aligning India's practice with the U.S. human-authorship requirement.
* **The Copyright Act, 1957 [CONFIRMED_PRIMARY]:**
  * **Section 2(d)(vi):** Defines the "author" of a computer-generated work as "the person who causes the work to be created". However, this 1994 amendment was designed for computer-assisted tools, and its application to autonomous generative AI remains untested by Indian courts.
  * **Originality Standard:** Under *Eastern Book Company v. D.B. Modak* (2008), the Supreme Court of India established that copyright requires a "modicum of creativity" born of human skill and judgment. Purely autonomous AI outputs with minimal human input likely fail this threshold.
* ***ANI Media Pvt. Ltd. v. OpenAI Inc.* (Delhi High Court) [CONFIRMED_PRIMARY]:**
  * **July 24, 2026:** Justice Amit Bansal delivered a landmark 135-page interim order declining to grant an interim injunction to news agency ANI, which had sued OpenAI for INR 20 million (approx. USD 210,000) in damages for unauthorized training and hallucinations.
  * **The Ruling:** The Delhi High Court held that, prima facie, OpenAI's storage of copyrighted news content to train ChatGPT falls under the "fair dealing" exception of Section 52(1)(a)(i) of the Copyright Act, 1957 (protecting fair dealing for "private or personal use, including research").
  * **Commercial Use:** The court rejected ANI's argument that commercial AI platforms cannot invoke this exception, noting that the Indian legislature only explicitly barred commercial exploitation in other specific subsections of Section 52.
  * **RAG Technology:** The court noted that real-time responses generated using Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) act like a search engine and do not extract stored copies from training data.
* **DPDP Act, 2023 Compliance [CONFIRMED_PRIMARY]:**
  * The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, and its corresponding Rules (notified on November 13, 2025) require all organizations in India to be compliant by May 13, 2027.
  * This adds a strict compliance layer for AI training datasets containing Personally Identifiable Information (PII), with penalties running up to ₹250 crore per breach category.

## 2. The Counterarguments & Practical Realities
* **The Boundary/Enforceability Problem:** Modern software development is a hybrid blend of human writing, AI autocompletion, and agentic scaffolding [REPORTED_SECONDARY]. There is no metadata layer that tracks authorship line-by-line, and refactoring quickly destroys any trace of who wrote what [REPORTED_SECONDARY]. Enforcing "all AI-generated code must be open source" is practically impossible unless it translates to "all code touched by AI must be open source"—which would cover almost all modern software [REPORTED_SECONDARY].
* **The "Learning" Analogy:** Critics on Hacker News argue that human developers also train on open-source code [CONFIRMED_PRIMARY]. If a human learns programming by reading GPL-licensed code and subsequently writes proprietary code, they are not violating the GPL (unless they copy-paste code verbatim) [CONFIRMED_PRIMARY]. Why should an artificial neural network be treated differently? [CONFIRMED_PRIMARY].
* **Voluntary Compliance Deficit:** Private enterprises are highly unlikely to voluntarily open-source their proprietary codebases simply because they used GitHub Copilot or Cursor [REPORTED_SECONDARY].

## 1. Legal & Regulatory Framework: AI Copyright & Training in India

Unlike the United States, which strictly enforces a human-authorship requirement (e.g., *Thaler v. Perlmutter*), India's copyright regime provides a unique statutory pathway for computer-generated works, though it is heavily constrained by originality standards and recent judicial precedents.

### Statutory Provisions (Copyright Act, 1957)
*   **Section 2(d)(vi):** Explicitly defines the "author" of a computer-generated literary, dramatic, musical, or artistic work as **"the person who causes the work to be created"**. This differs fundamentally from the US stance by allowing a human to claim authorship over machine-generated outputs.
*   **Section 2(o):** Classifies computer programs, tables, compilations, and databases under the definition of "literary works," making software code eligible for copyright protection under this framework.
*   **Originality Threshold:** Under the Indian Supreme Court's landmark ruling in ***Eastern Book Company v. D.B. Modak* (2008)**, copyright only subsists if a work reflects a "modicum of creativity" and goes beyond a purely mechanical exercise. Consequently, code generated by entering a simple prompt into an AI model with minimal human modification is unlikely to meet this threshold.
*   *Tag:* **CONFIRMED_PRIMARY** (Statute and Supreme Court precedent).

### Landmark Precedent: *ANI Media Pvt. Ltd. v. OpenAI OpCo LLC* (Delhi High Court)
*   **The Dispute:** Filed in November 2024, news agency ANI alleged that OpenAI copied and stored its copyrighted news content without authorization to train ChatGPT, and that ChatGPT's outputs reproduced its reporting.
*   **The Interim Order (July 24, 2026):** Justice Amit Bansal of the Delhi High Court declined to grant an interim injunction to ANI, delivering India's first substantive ruling on AI training and copyright.
*   **Fair Dealing for AI Training:** The Court held, *prima facie*, that storing copyrighted works to train LLMs qualifies as **"fair dealing"** under **Section 52(1)(a)(i)** of the Copyright Act, 1957 (which covers "private or personal use, including research").
*   **The Two-Step Enquiry:**
    1.  *Purpose Test:* The Court ruled that AI training constitutes "research" (not limited to human research, extending to machine learning) and is an internal, private process.
    2.  *Fairness Test:* The Court found that OpenAI's use was limited to training (no evidence of systematic memorization or verbatim republication), there was no market substitution shown, and AI serves a substantial public interest.
*   **Jurisdiction:** The Court rejected OpenAI's argument that Indian courts lack jurisdiction because its servers are in the US, holding that the copying begins with accessing copyrighted works from India.
*   **Appeal Status:** ANI appealed the order. On **September 15, 2026**, a Division Bench of the Delhi High Court declined to grant immediate interim relief and scheduled the appeal for further hearing on **December 8, 2026**.
*   *Tag:* **CONFIRMED_PRIMARY** (Delhi High Court orders: CS(COMM) 1028/2024).

### Indian Copyright Office (ICO) Stance on AI Authorship
*   **The RAGHAV Case (2020-2021):** The ICO initially registered an artwork listing the AI tool "RAGHAV" (Random Art Generator and Human Aided Visualisation) as a co-author alongside a human. However, in December 2021, the ICO issued a withdrawal notice, clarifying that an AI tool cannot legally be an "author".
*   **The DABUS Case (September 2026):** The ICO ruled on Stephen Thaler's application for the artwork *A Recent Entrance to Paradise*. The ICO held that an AI system (DABUS) cannot be recognized as an author under the Copyright Act, but did not reject the possibility of copyright protection for the work itself. The application failed procedurally because it named DABUS as the author instead of the human (Dr. Stephen Thaler) who "caused the work to be created".
*   *Tag:* **CONFIRMED_PRIMARY** (Indian Copyright Office rulings).
