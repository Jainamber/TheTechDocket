# Global AI-Training Copyright Law Context — as of July 25, 2026

Research compiled for coverage of the July 24, 2026 Delhi High Court ruling (Justice Amit Bansal) denying ANI's interim injunction against OpenAI, holding ChatGPT's training on/storage of ANI content is prima facie covered by Section 52(1)(a) "fair dealing." This document collects the worldwide comparison context. Every item is dated and sourced; items confirmed in 2+ independent sources are marked **[2+ sources]**; items found in only one source are marked **[single-source]**; explicit numeric/date conflicts between sources are flagged as **[CONFLICT]**.

## 0. The India news hook itself (for reference/context)

- **ANI Media Pvt Ltd v. OpenAI, Delhi High Court** — filed Nov/Dec 2024 by Asian News International, India's first generative-AI copyright infringement suit.
- **July 24, 2026**: Justice Amit Bansal denied ANI's application for an **interim injunction** (this is an interlocutory order, NOT a final merits ruling). Reasoning: OpenAI's storage of ANI's works falls under Section 52(1)(a) Copyright Act (fair dealing for research/private use), and ChatGPT's generated outputs were found "not substantially similar" to ANI's original works, so no prima facie infringement under Section 51. OpenAI had separately argued jurisdiction (training/servers outside India); the court proceeded to rule on the merits notwithstanding. **[2+ sources]**
  - Source: LiveLaw (https://www.livelaw.in/high-court/delhi-high-court/delhi-high-court-openai-ani-copyright-infringement-interim-relief-chatgpt-542746), July 24, 2026
  - Corroborating headlines: Business Standard, Free Press Journal, Adgully, Telangana Today (all July 24, 2026) — see Source URLs list.
- Other Indian media/rights-holder organizations have sought impleadment/intervention in the broader dispute, variously reported as including the Federation of Indian Publishers, NDTV, Hindustan Times, Indian Express, T-Series, Saregama, and Sony Music — exact list varies slightly by source. **[2+ sources, list details vary]**
  - Sources: TechDodo (https://techdodo.in/articles/ai-copyright-lawsuit-2025-india); Bar & Bench (https://www.barandbench.com/view-point/ani-v-openai-is-a-copyright-case-its-also-a-masterclass-in-how-mandates-are-lost), March 2026

## 1. United States

### NYT (and other publishers) v. OpenAI / Microsoft — MDL, SDNY, Judge Sidney Stein
- Filed December 2023. Motion to dismiss copyright claims **denied** March 2025 — direct and contributory infringement claims proceed; court cited "numerous and well-publicized instances" of ChatGPT reproducing Times-derived content. **[2+ sources]**
- **January 2026**: Judge Stein affirmed an order requiring OpenAI to produce **20 million de-identified ChatGPT conversation logs** (scaled down from an original ask of 120 million), holding output logs are "relevant to OpenAI's fair use defense" even absent direct Times content. **[2+ sources]**
- **April 2, 2026**: Summary judgment briefing concluded. No trial date set; SJ ruling expected **Q3 2026**; potential trial in 2027 if claims survive.
- **April 2026**: Court granted additional deposition time after finding an OpenAI corporate (30(b)(6)) witness inadequately prepared, ordering re-designation of a better-prepared witness.
- **July 9, 2026**: NYT and other publishers filed a memorandum seeking **sanctions against OpenAI for allegedly withholding discovery evidence** on training-data sourcing, filtering, and verification processes — filing coincided with OpenAI's launch of "ChatGPT Work" and GPT-5.6. **[single-source, truescho.com — treat cautiously]**
- Damages sought described as "billions of dollars"; statutory damages theoretically capped at $150,000/work for willful infringement.
- Court also rejected plaintiffs' bid to obtain Microsoft's Bing search index data as part of discovery; class plaintiffs gained added interrogatories re: OpenAI's LibGen-sourced book downloads.
- **[CONFLICT]** Number of consolidated cases in the MDL: one source (axis-intelligence.com) says **16 consolidated cases**; another (McKool Smith) says **12 consolidated cases**. Could reflect different snapshot dates as new cases are added to the MDL — treat the exact count as unsettled.
- Sources: LegalClarity (https://legalclarity.org/new-york-times-vs-openai-lawsuit-status-and-timeline/); ailawsuittracker.com/cases/new-york-times-v-openai/; McKool Smith AI Litigation Update Nov 24 2025 (https://www.mckoolsmith.com/newsroom-ailitigation-46); truescho.com (https://truescho.com/en/blog/openai-nyt-copyright-evidence-2026); axis-intelligence.com tracker

### Bartz v. Anthropic — N.D. Cal.
- **June 23, 2025**: Judge **William Alsup** issued a partial summary judgment fair-use ruling with three distinct holdings **[2+ sources]**:
  1. Training LLMs on copyrighted books = fair use ("exceedingly transformative," produces new text, not a public redistribution of the originals).
  2. Destructively scanning **legally purchased** print books into a digital library = fair use (transformative format change).
  3. Downloading and retaining **pirated/unauthorized** copies of over **7 million books** (even for eventual transformative training use) = **NOT fair use** — "there is no workaround in the Copyright Act that allows AI companies to avoid paying for material that they could lawfully obtain."
  - Source: Akin Gump (https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/ai-law-and-regulation-tracker/district-court-rules-ai-training-can-be-fair-use-in-bartz-v-anthropic)
  - It was the piracy holding (#3) that exposed Anthropic to massive statutory-damages liability and drove the settlement.
- **December 2025**: Judge Alsup retired; case reassigned to Judge **Araceli Martínez-Olguín**. **[2+ sources: chatgptiseatingtheworld.com, dailyjournal.com, authorsalliance.org]**
- **July 20, 2026** (one source says July 21 — **[CONFLICT, minor]**): Judge Martínez-Olguín granted **final approval** of the settlement.
  - **Total settlement: $1.5 billion** — described as the largest copyright class-action recovery to date. **[2+ sources: Authors Guild, JURIST, Locus, Publishers Lunch, chatgptiseatingtheworld.com]**
  - **Per-work payout: approximately $3,000/work** (described as "roughly four times the statutory minimum for ordinary infringement"), covering **over 400,000 books**; one tracker gives a precise claimed-works figure of **447,576 works [single-source, axis-intelligence.com]**.
  - Covers Anthropic's acquisition of pirated books through **August 25, 2025**; authors retain rights to sue separately over AI *outputs* and future conduct.
  - Class counsel fees **cut to $101,561,111** (from $187.5 million requested) — court applied a 3.75x multiplier to a $27,082,963 lodestar instead of the 6.92x sought, citing Ninth Circuit precedent that "multipliers generally range from 1 to 4."
  - Special Master **Theodore K. Cheng** appointed to resolve claimant disputes; one source states **>91% of eligible authors** filed claims **[single-source, chatgptiseatingtheworld.com]**.
  - Sources: Authors Guild (https://authorsguild.org/news/court-grants-final-approval-anthropic-copyright-settlement/); JURIST (https://www.jurist.org/news/2026/07/judge-approves-record-1-5-billion-settlement-involving-anthropic/); chatgptiseatingtheworld.com (https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/2026/07/20/judge-martinez-olguin-approves-historic-bartz-v-anthropic-class-action-settlement-but-reduces-attorneys-fee-award-to-101561111-instead-of-187500000-sought/); Publishers Lunch; Locus; AAP; Authors Alliance

### Kadrey v. Meta — N.D. Cal., Judge Vince Chhabria
- **June 25, 2025** (two days after the Alsup Anthropic ruling): Chhabria granted **summary judgment for Meta**, finding the training use "highly transformative" under fair-use factor one. **[2+ sources: Goodwin, Copyright Alliance, FisherBroyles, Perkins Coie]**
- Explicitly and deliberately **narrow**: "this ruling does not stand for the proposition that Meta's use of copyrighted materials to train its language models is lawful. It stands only for the proposition that these plaintiffs made the wrong arguments and failed to develop a record in support of the right one." Applies only to the 13 named plaintiffs — **not a class action**, so no broad precedent for other Meta training uses.
- Chhabria flagged that a properly-argued **"market dilution"** theory (AI-generated works flooding and devaluing the market for human-created works, especially for lesser-known/emerging authors) could defeat fair use in a future case with a better record — an explicitly open question. He also suggested cases involving **news articles** might present stronger anti-fair-use arguments than the book-plaintiffs here.
- As of 2026, a separate BitTorrent/**"seeding" piracy claim** in the same case remains live (i.e., the fair-use win did not end the case). **[single-source, axis-intelligence.com]**
- Sources: Goodwin Law (https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/06/alerts-practices-aiml-northern-district-of-california-judge-rules); Copyright Alliance (https://copyrightalliance.org/kadrey-v-meta-decision/); FisherBroyles; Perkins Coie; Akin Gump

### Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence — D. Del., Judge Stephanos Bibas
- **February 2025**: On summary judgment, the court held Ross's use of ~2,000+ Westlaw headnotes to train a competing legal-search AI was **NOT fair use** (reversing Bibas's own earlier tentative 2023 view). **[2+ sources: Loeb, Jenner & Block, Venable, Goodwin, DWT]**
  - Factor 1 (purpose/character): favors Thomson Reuters — commercial and directly competitive; rejected the "intermediate copying" defense; distinguished *Google v. Oracle* because headnotes, unlike APIs, don't serve a comparable functional-interoperability purpose.
  - Factor 2 (nature of work): favors Ross — headnotes are only thinly creative.
  - Factor 3 (amount/substantiality): favors Ross — headnotes never appeared in user-facing output.
  - Factor 4 (market effect) — the court called this "the single most important element": decisively favors Thomson Reuters. Ross's tool functioned as a market substitute for Westlaw, harming licensing opportunities (including for AI-training data specifically).
- **On appeal**: Third Circuit — oral argument held **June 11, 2026**, described as "the first federal appellate case to squarely address" AI-training fair use. Panel discussion centered on whether Ross's use was a mere "cheat sheet" vs. a "materially different" technology, and on how to define the relevant market (standalone headnotes market vs. Westlaw/AI-training-licensing market). **No ruling issued yet as of July 2026**; decision expected late 2026. **[2+ sources: Baker Botts, aicounsel.substack.com, legalai.substack.com, Chat GPT Is Eating the World]**
- Sources: Loeb & Loeb (https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2025/02/thomson-reuters-v-ross-intelligence-inc); Baker Botts (https://www.bakerbotts.com/thought-leadership/publications/2026/july/third-circuit-hears-oral-argument); Third Circuit docket via CourtListener (25-2153)

## 2. United Kingdom

### Getty Images v. Stability AI — English High Court
- **Judgment: November 4, 2025**, neutral citation **[2025] EWHC 2863 (Ch)**, **Mrs Justice Joanna Smith DBE**, Business and Property Courts (Chancery Division, IP List). **[2+ sources, incl. official judiciary.uk record]**
- **What Getty lost:**
  - Primary copyright infringement and database-right claims over *training* — **abandoned by Getty before trial**, conceding no evidence the training itself occurred in the UK.
  - Secondary copyright infringement (the theory that the Stable Diffusion model itself is an "infringing copy" under UK law) — **rejected**. Court held the model weights do not store or reproduce Getty's copyrighted works: "the Model itself does not store any of those Copyright Works; the model weights are not themselves an infringing copy."
  - Trade mark dilution claim (s.10(3) Trade Marks Act 1994) — failed entirely.
- **What Getty won (partially):**
  - **Trade mark infringement** under sections 10(1) and 10(2) Trade Marks Act 1994 — for a limited number of instances where Stable Diffusion outputs reproduced Getty watermarks identical/confusingly similar to Getty's registered marks. Explicitly **not widespread**, and not established for later model versions.
- **Appeal**: Getty was **granted permission to appeal the secondary-copyright-infringement findings in December 2025**. **[2+ sources: Mondaq, HSF Kramer, corroborated by ailawsuittracker.com]**
- Sources: Bird & Bird (https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/uk/stability-ai-defeats-getty-images-copyright-claims-in-first-of-its-kind-dispute-before-the-high-cour); Judiciary UK official judgment page (https://www.judiciary.uk/judgments/getty-images-v-stability-ai/); Mondaq (https://www.mondaq.com/uk/trademark/1725006/getty-granted-permission-to-appeal-secondary-copyright-infringement-findings-from-getty-v-stability-ai); HSF Kramer (https://www.hsfkramer.com/notes/ip/2025-12/getty-granted-permission-to-appeal-secondary-copyright-infringement-findings-in-getty-v-stability-ai); Mayer Brown; Milbank

### UK government TDM/AI-copyright policy — status as of 2026
- **March 2026**: UK government published its **"Report on Copyright and Artificial Intelligence"**, the outcome of the long-running consultation. **[2+ sources: Birketts, Simmons & Simmons, Reed Smith, Lewis Silkin]**
- The government **declined to adopt any of the four previously floated policy options**, including the previously-favored **"Option 3"** — a broad TDM exception with a rights-holder **opt-out** mechanism. The opt-out proposal is described by commentators as effectively **dead/abandoned**.
- Reasons cited: implementation/monitoring burden on small creators, AI-developer uncertainty about continuously re-licensing as new opt-outs appear, and unproven real-world effectiveness of opt-out tooling. Creative-industry consultation responses reportedly showed only ~3% support for the opt-out option; licensing-based approaches drew much stronger support.
- **Net effect: status quo maintained** — commercial AI training on copyrighted works in the UK still generally requires a licence (no blanket non-consensual TDM exception for commercial use); the government set **no legislative timeline**, saying it "would not legislate until it was confident that reforms would meet its objectives," and will keep monitoring EU/California developments and gathering evidence on narrower targeted exceptions (research, public interest).
- Criticized by **Lord Holmes of Richmond MBE** as amounting to "no decision and more consultations."
- Sources: Birketts (https://www.birketts.co.uk/legal-update/copyright-and-artificial-intelligence-uk-government-report-march-2026/); Simmons & Simmons (https://www.simmons-simmons.com/en/publications/cmn8tmopv0050ustoq2e6ndz2/a-masterclass-in-can-kicking-uk-government-views-on-ai-and-copyright); Reed Smith; official report PDF (https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69ba692226909a14239612e4/CP2602959_-_Report_on_Copyright_and_Artificial_Intelligence_web.pdf); Lewis Silkin; Hogan Lovells; Pinsent Masons

## 3. European Union

### EU AI Act — Article 53 GPAI obligations & link to DSM Directive Article 4
- **Article 53(1)(c)** of the EU AI Act requires providers of general-purpose AI (GPAI) models to "put in place a policy to comply with Union law on copyright and related rights, and in particular to identify and comply with, including through state-of-the-art technologies, a reservation of rights expressed pursuant to **Article 4(3) of Directive (EU) 2019/790**" (the DSM Directive). **[single-source for the precise legal-text framing, artificialintelligenceact.eu — but this is the official/authoritative consolidated-text tracking site]**
- This directly incorporates the **DSM Directive's Article 4 TDM exception**, which allows commercial text-and-data-mining **unless** the rightsholder has opted out ("reserved rights") in a machine-readable or equivalent manner — a mechanism that predates the AI Act (DSM Directive dates to 2019).
- GPAI providers must use "state-of-the-art technologies" to detect and honor such opt-outs.
- The **open-source exemption** in Article 53(2) does **not** excuse copyright-policy compliance; it only waives certain technical-documentation obligations for freely-licensed models that pose no systemic risk.
- Article 53 obligations entered into force/became applicable **August 2, 2025**.
- There is also a **GPAI Code of Practice** process addressing TDM "protocols" (rights-reservation technical implementation) — referenced in a December 2025 EU regulatory item, but not independently verified in depth here. **[single-source, regulations.ai — flagged, not deeply verified]**
- Sources: artificialintelligenceact.eu Article 53 (https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/53/); Clifford Chance (https://www.cliffordchance.com/insights/resources/blogs/ip-insights/2025/10/copyright-compliance-under-the-eu-ai-act-for-gpai-model-providers.html); Regulations.ai (https://regulations.ai/regulations/eu-2025-12-tdm-protocols-ai-act-gpai)

### GEMA v. OpenAI — Munich Regional Court I (Landgericht München I)
- **Case No. 42 O 14139/24. Ruling date: November 11, 2025.** **[2+ sources broadly corroborate "November 2025"; the specific Nov 11 date comes from cms.law]**
- The court found OpenAI liable on copyright grounds for:
  - **Memorization** — storing protected song lyrics within model parameters, classified as **reproduction** under §16 German Copyright Act (UrhG).
  - **Regurgitation** — the model's ability to output those lyrics on simple prompting, found to violate reproduction rights, adaptation rights (§23 UrhG), and the right of public communication (§19a UrhG).
- Significantly, this ruling addressed acts **subsequent to training** (storage/output), distinguishing it from an earlier **Hamburg Regional Court** ruling (GEMA/Kneschke v. LAION, September 2024) that dealt with the lawfulness of compiling the training dataset itself.
- The court granted injunctive relief, an information-disclosure order, and upheld a damages claim (specific damages figure not stated in sources reviewed).
- **Not final** — the court itself anticipated the ruling "will be subject to comprehensive review in the appeal proceedings." As of July 2026, the case is tracked as having a **German appeal pending**. **[2+ sources: CMS Law, Norton Rose Fulbright, axis-intelligence.com tracker]**
- Sources: CMS (https://cms.law/en/deu/legal-updates/gema-vs.-openai-munich-regional-court-i-issues-landmark-copyright-decision); Norton Rose Fulbright (https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/656613b2/germany-delivers-landmark-copyright-ruling-against-openai-what-it-means-for-ai-and-ip); Bird & Bird; Heuking; Preu Bohlig

## 4. Japan

### Copyright Act Article 30-4 — the "machine learning paradise"
- Article 30-4 (effective from the January 1, 2019 Copyright Act amendment) permits using copyrighted works for "information analysis" — including AI/ML training — **without the rightsholder's permission**, and unlike the EU/UK frameworks, this is **not limited to non-commercial use**; Japan's Ministry of Education (MEXT) has been quoted as saying AI companies can use "whatever they want" for training "regardless of whether it is for non-profit or commercial purposes." **[single-source for the direct ministerial quote, Lexology — but the broader Art. 30-4 commercial-scope point is corroborated in multiple secondary-source summaries]**
- The provision in principle can extend even to training on **pirated/illegally-sourced** content, which is the core reason for the "paradise for machine learning" label — a deliberate policy choice tied to Japan's push for AI-driven productivity amid an aging population.
- **Key limiting doctrine — "non-enjoyment purpose":** Article 30-4 does not apply where the purpose is to let a person "enjoy the thoughts or sentiments expressed" in the work. Japan's Agency for Cultural Affairs ("General Understanding on AI and Copyright in Japan," 2024) draft guidance identifies specific **prohibited/limited scenarios**, including:
  - Fine-tuning intended to generate outputs similar to specific copyrighted works in the training set.
  - Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) set-ups that output a work's protected creative expression.
  - Deliberate imitation of a specific creator's distinctive style.
  - A **market-harm proviso**: non-enjoyment uses are still restricted where they would "unreasonably prejudice" the rightsholder's interests — e.g., reproducing a licensable database product without payment, circumventing technical protection measures (including robots.txt), or competing with commercial data-analysis products.
  - Knowingly using pirated source material can still expose a developer to **contributory infringement** liability.
- **[2+ sources: Lexology "Japan a Paradise for Machine Learning, Not Generative AI"; Agency for Cultural Affairs official English-language PDF]**
- Sources: Lexology (https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=d6358d5b-b020-430e-84b9-dd3497822c9b); Agency for Cultural Affairs / bunka.go.jp official overview PDF (https://www.bunka.go.jp/english/policy/copyright/pdf/94055801_01.pdf); WIPO document by Tatsuhiro Ueno

## 5. India — comparative doctrine and policy context

### Structural comparison: Section 52 "fair dealing" (India) vs. §107 "fair use" (US)
- **India (closed/enumerated model):** Section 52(1)(a) of the Copyright Act, 1957 permits fair dealing only for specifically listed purposes — **criticism or review; private/personal use including research; and reporting of current affairs.** Indian courts, including the Delhi High Court itself, have held this list is **exhaustive, not illustrative**. In *Super Cassettes Industries v. Chintamani Rao*, the Delhi High Court stated that "Section 52 carefully and exhaustively enlists various actions which would not constitute infringement," and that expanding the list to cover new technology is a job for **Parliament, not the courts**. **[2+ sources: Bar & Bench, ManagingIP]**
- **US (open-ended model):** §107 fair use is a flexible four-factor balancing test (purpose/character of use — including the judge-made "transformative use" doctrine; nature of the copyrighted work; amount/substantiality used; effect on the market) with **no closed list of qualifying purposes**. This is the doctrinal tool that let Alsup and Chhabria rule for the AI companies in 2025 (see Section 1).
- **Expert characterization (for the article):** Shubhi Priyadarshi, writing in Bar & Bench, argues AI training fails Indian copyright analysis at the **threshold "purpose test"** rather than at a fairness-balancing stage: because Section 52(1)(a)'s "research" prong contemplates a human engaging with a work's *meaning*, whereas AI training "converts [works] into numerical representations to enable pattern recognition" — arguably not "research" in the doctrinal sense at all — the fairness analysis is never reached. She contrasts this with the US's "adaptable framework" and warns against "importing US concepts like 'transformative use'" into a structurally different Indian statute. **[single-source expert view, but the underlying doctrinal facts about Section 52's exhaustive/enumerated character are independently corroborated]**
  - Source: Bar & Bench (https://www.barandbench.com/columns/fair-dealing-cannot-be-presumed-why-ai-training-fails-the-purpose-test-under-indian-copyright-law)
- India currently has **no dedicated text-and-data-mining (TDM) exception** at all — unlike Japan's Article 30-4, the EU's DSM Directive Article 4, or (per the June 2025 US rulings) an emerging fair-use pathway. Parliament has reportedly taken the position that the existing 1957 Act is "well-equipped" and that commercial generative-AI use requires a license unless it independently falls within a Section 52 exception. **[single-source paraphrase of a parliamentary statement, via ManagingIP]**
  - Source: ManagingIP (https://www.managingip.com/article/2f3huy6goqztxnabhdvy8/sponsored-content/ani-v-openai-generative-ais-use-of-copyrighted-works-under-indian-law)

### India government/policy activity on AI and copyright
- **MeitY "Report on AI Governance Guidelines Development"** (released **January 2025**): India's first major whole-of-government AI policy document. On copyright specifically, it flags (i) that using copyrighted data to train AI models commercially may **exceed** the Copyright Act's fair-dealing/research exceptions, since those exceptions are geared to personal/individual research rather than institutional or commercial use, and (ii) that AI-generated outputs raise unresolved **copyrightability** questions given Indian law's human-authorship requirement. It calls for "clear policy guidance and potential legal reforms" without resolving them itself, and proposes broader governance architecture — an **Inter-Ministerial AI Coordination Committee**, a **Technical Secretariat within MeitY**, and an **AI Sub-Group** — none of which is explicitly assigned copyright as its lead issue. **[2+ sources: Securiti.ai, AZB Partners]**
  - Sources: Securiti.ai (https://securiti.ai/ai-governance-in-india-meity-2025-report/); AZB Partners (https://www.azbpartners.com/bank/update-on-meitys-report-on-ai-governance-guidelines-development/)
- **DPIIT "Working Paper on Generative AI and Copyright"** (**December 2025**) — the more copyright-specific policy document, with **MeitY publicly backing** its approach. It proposes a **hybrid statutory-licensing model**: AI developers could train on copyrighted works without needing prior permission, but would owe **revenue-sharing** once use/systems cross a prescribed threshold — reframing copyright, for AI-training purposes, from an exclusionary right into a **remuneration right**, to be administered by a proposed **"Copyright Regulatory and Compensation Authority for Training" (CRCAT)**. MeitY explicitly rejected both a pure "lawful access, no licence needed" position (advocated by industry groups such as the Business Software Alliance, which frames training as extracting non-copyrightable statistical patterns) and a pure individual-consent licensing regime, stating "Public good, while a worthy objective, cannot become a qualifying criterion for innovation, or unconditional immunity against legal challenges." Open questions flagged: how to value works for training-remuneration purposes, CRCAT's precise authority, and interplay with the DPDP Act. **[2+ sources: MediaNama, Mondaq]**
  - Sources: MediaNama (https://www.medianama.com/2025/12/223-meity-mandatory-ai-copyright-licensing-tech-industry-lawful-access/); Mondaq (https://www.mondaq.com/india/copyright/1728628/the-dpiit-working-paper-on-ai-and-copyright-regulatory-signals-and-practical-implications); working paper itself hosted at dpiit.gov.in (https://www.dpiit.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/12/ff266bbeed10c48e3479c941484f3525.pdf)
- **DPDP Act, 2023 relevance:** Commentators flag a structural tension rather than a resolved rule: AI training datasets commonly mix copyrighted works with **personal data** (images, speech, behavioural data), and the DPDP Act's consent/withdrawal framework sits uneasily against copyright's stable, long-duration property model. The specific unresolved problem is a **"machine unlearning" gap** — no settled legal or technical mechanism exists to retroactively purge a trained model's parameters of a data principal's contribution after they withdraw consent, and it's unclear whether such withdrawal would even affect IP claims in the resulting model. Commentators call for legislative harmonization rather than leaving this to case-by-case litigation. **[single-source depth, Mondaq — general tension is plausible/consistent with global DPDP commentary but the specific "unlearning" framing is only from one fetched source]**
  - Source: Mondaq (https://www.mondaq.com/india/data-protection/1740738/the-digital-personal-data-protection-act-2023-and-intellectual-property-law-an-emerging-conflict-in-indias-data-driven-economy)

## Cross-cutting comparative synthesis (for article framing)

| Jurisdiction | Governing doctrine | Commercial AI training generally permitted? | 2025-2026 signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| US | §107 fair use (open 4-factor test) | Contested/mixed — 2 rulings for AI companies on *training* (Alsup/Anthropic, Chhabria/Meta, both June 2025), 1 against on a *competing-product* use (Bibas/Thomson Reuters, Feb 2025, on appeal) | Piracy of source copies (not the training itself) is where liability actually landed — Anthropic's $1.5B settlement was about *how it acquired* the books, not the training use |
| UK | No general TDM exception; abandoned opt-out proposal (March 2026) | No — licensing generally required; status quo | Getty's *training* claim collapsed for lack of evidence of UK-based training, not because training was found lawful |
| EU | DSM Directive Art. 4 TDM exception with opt-out, enforced via AI Act Art. 53 GPAI obligations | Yes, unless rightsholder opts out | GEMA v. OpenAI (Munich, Nov 2025) shows liability can still attach post-training (storage/output), separate from the TDM-training question |
| Japan | Copyright Act Art. 30-4 | Yes, broadly, including commercial use and even some pirated-source ingestion, subject to a market-harm/non-enjoyment proviso | Most permissive major-economy regime; recent guidance narrows the output/enjoyment side, not the training/input side |
| India | Section 52(1)(a) fair dealing — closed enumerated list, no TDM exception | Unsettled/contested — Delhi HC's July 24, 2026 interim (not final) ruling read "research/private use" broadly enough to cover OpenAI's storage of ANI content prima facie | Policy track (DPIIT/MeitY) is simultaneously moving toward a statutory licensing/remuneration model (CRCAT proposal, Dec 2025) — suggesting the legislature, not just the courts, expects to resolve this |
