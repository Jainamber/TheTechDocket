## 1. CBP Official Policy — Directive 3340-049A (and its January 2026 successor, 3340-049B)

### IMPORTANT CAVEAT FOR THE WRITER
CBP appears to have **superseded Directive 3340-049A with Directive 3340-049B, effective January 2026** — six months before this news peg. The core basic/advanced framework is unchanged, but the definition of "electronic device" was expanded. Do not cite 3340-049A as "current policy" without noting it was replaced. See section 1c.

### 1a. Basic vs. Advanced Search (text of 3340-049A, confirmed from an authentic court-hosted copy of the CBP directive)
**[PRIMARY]** Source: CBP Directive 3340-049A, "Border Search of Electronic Media," as filed/hosted by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the First Circuit: https://www.ca1.uscourts.gov/sites/ca1/files/citations/CBP-Directive-3340-049A-Border-Search-of-Electronic-Media-Compliant.pdf
Cross-confirmed by CBP's own summary page: https://cbp.gov/travel/cbp-search-authority/border-search-electronic-devices **[PRIMARY]**

- **Basic Search (§5.1.3):** Officers may examine devices and "review and analyze information encountered at the border" **with or without suspicion** during routine inspections — manual review, no external equipment.
- **Advanced Search (§5.1.4):** Defined as connecting "external equipment...to an electronic device" to "review, copy, and/or analyze its contents." Requires **"reasonable suspicion of activity in violation of the laws enforced or administered by CBP, or...a national security concern,"** PLUS **supervisory approval at the Grade 14 level or higher.**
- **Warrant requirement: NONE.** The directive cites *United States v. Montoya de Hernandez* for the proposition that "Routine searches...are not subject to any requirement of reasonable suspicion, probable cause, or warrant."
- **Device detention (§5.4.1.1):** Standard detention should not exceed **five (5) days**; extensions beyond 5 days need Port Director approval; extensions beyond 15 days need Director of Field Operations approval, in 7-day increments.
- **Data destruction (§5.4.1.2):** Copies of data must be destroyed within **7 days** if no probable cause is found, or **no later than 21 days** if additional time is justified.
- **Passcodes (§5.3.1–5.3.3):** Travelers are "obligated to present electronic devices...in a condition that allows inspection"; officers "may request the individual's assistance in presenting" access. Passcodes are to be "deleted or destroyed when no longer needed" (§5.3.2). A device that remains passcode-locked because the traveler won't assist **can be detained "pending a determination as to its admissibility, exclusion, or other disposition"** (§5.3.3) — i.e., non-cooperation does not stop the search, it just delays it and puts the device at risk of prolonged seizure.

### 1b. cbp.gov's own plain-language summary (cross-check)
**[PRIMARY]** https://cbp.gov/travel/cbp-search-authority/border-search-electronic-devices
- "Basic Search: Any border search of an electronic device that is not an advanced search," manual review, no external equipment.
- "Advanced Search: Occurs when an officer connects external equipment to an electronic device" to review/copy/analyze; requires reasonable suspicion of a law violation or national security concern, plus senior-manager (GS-14+) approval.
- Confirms: passcodes are deleted after use; CBP cannot compel access to remote/cloud-only data and is supposed to place the device in airplane mode/disable network connectivity before a search (to avoid pulling cloud content); U.S. citizens cannot be denied entry for refusing device inspection, though devices may be detained; non-citizens' refusal "may affect admissibility decisions."

### 1c. The update: CBP Directive 3340-049B (effective January 2026)
**[SECONDARY]** Law-firm analysis: https://www.mltaikins.com/insights/u-s-customs-and-border-protection-updates-border-search-of-electronic-devices-directive/
Primary document exists at cbp.gov but returned a 403 error on fetch (URL confirmed via search results, could not verify text directly): https://www.cbp.gov/sites/default/files/2026-01/cbp_directive_3340-049b_jan_2026_508.pdf and https://www.cbp.gov/document/directives/cbp-directive-no-3340-049b-border-search-electronic-devices **[PRIMARY — URL confirmed real, content UNVERIFIED by direct fetch]**

- Effective **January 2026**.
- **Expanded "electronic device" definition** to explicitly include flash drives, SIM cards, GPS units, drones/UAS, vehicle infotainment systems, and **smartwatches/wearables** — none of which were explicitly enumerated in 3340-049A.
- Basic/advanced search structure and suspicion thresholds **carry over unchanged**: basic = no suspicion; advanced = reasonable suspicion of a CBP-enforced law violation or national security concern + supervisory approval.
- Retains the same passcode-deletion and ~21-day data-destruction-absent-probable-cause language.
- This lines up with the FY2025 statistics (below), which for the first time break out **smartwatch** searches — see VisaHQ coverage: https://www.visahq.com/news/2026-03-23/us/cbp-electronic-device-searches-jump-17-percent-wearables-now-in-scope/ **[SECONDARY]**

## 3. Rights by Immigration Status

### U.S. Citizens
- **Cannot be denied re-entry / refused admission for declining to unlock a device or provide a passcode.** **[PRIMARY]** cbp.gov: https://cbp.gov/travel/cbp-search-authority/border-search-electronic-devices ; confirmed by NACDL primer **[SECONDARY]**: https://www.nacdl.org/getattachment/bb98e181-eae0-47cb-b4f1-4ac3f1634ab8/border-search-primer.pdf
- **Device CAN still be detained/seized** even though the person must be let in. Cannot be arrested merely for refusing.
- May face "prolonged secondary detention" as a practical consequence of refusal, per secondary legal-explainer sourcing (TechTimes, citing rights guides): https://www.techtimes.com/articles/321338/20260723/cbp-can-rifle-through-your-phone-hand-border-no-suspicion-required.htm **[SECONDARY]**

### Lawful Permanent Residents (green card holders)
- Treated closer to citizens than to visa holders but with **materially less certainty**. VisaVerge's rights explainer: LPRs "retain stronger protections than non-immigrants yet face significant risks... extended delays and potentially removal proceedings if officers view non-compliance as evidence of fraud." **[SECONDARY]** https://www.visaverge.com/travelrequirements/border-officers-can-search-your-phone-what-travelers-need-to-know/
- The NACDL primer is blunter: for LPRs there are "uncertain immigration risks in general" and it's "unclear what consequences may occur" — i.e., no settled rule. **[SECONDARY]** https://www.nacdl.org/getattachment/bb98e181-eae0-47cb-b4f1-4ac3f1634ab8/border-search-primer.pdf
- A dedicated immigration-law explainer on LPR port-of-entry rights confirmed the right to refuse to sign CBP paperwork but explicitly did **not** address device-refusal consequences for LPRs specifically — a genuine gap in publicly available guidance. **[SECONDARY]** https://www.passage.law/permanent-residents-know-your-rights-at-a-port-of-entry/

### Visa holders / visitors (non-immigrants)
- **CAN be denied entry / deemed inadmissible** for refusing to unlock a device or provide a passcode. **[PRIMARY, cbp.gov]** + **[SECONDARY corroboration]**
- VisaVerge: refusal "can lead to visa revocation, denial of entry or future ineligibility." Undocumented/other travelers face "immediate exclusion, detention or expedited removal." https://www.visaverge.com/travelrequirements/border-officers-can-search-your-phone-what-travelers-need-to-know/ **[SECONDARY]**
- NACDL: for non-citizens generally, refusal risks "detention, denial of entry, and deportation." **[SECONDARY]**

### Summary table
| Status | Denied entry for refusing? | Device seized if refuse? | Other consequences |
|---|---|---|---|
| U.S. citizen | No | Yes, can be detained | Prolonged secondary inspection; cannot be arrested for refusal alone |
| LPR / green card | Unsettled / fact-specific | Yes | Risk of removal proceedings if refusal is treated as fraud evidence; no clear rule |
| Visa holder / visitor | **Yes** | Yes | Visa revocation, inadmissibility, expedited removal |

## 4. Refusing to Give a Passcode — What Actually Happens

- **Device seizure length:** Directive default is **5 days**, extendable in CBP's discretion (Port Director sign-off past 5 days, Director of Field Operations past 15 days, in 7-day increments) — and multiple secondary sources note **there is no hard ceiling**; devices have "been held for weeks or months" in practice. **[PRIMARY]** (5-day/7-day/15-day structure from the directive text itself) + **[SECONDARY]** (real-world "weeks or months" claim): https://www.nacdl.org/getattachment/bb98e181-eae0-47cb-b4f1-4ac3f1634ab8/border-search-primer.pdf ; https://pacificlegal.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/SR-1299-Sweetland-Peikoff-and-Scacchi-Policy-Explainer-Searches-of-Electronic-Devices-at-Ports-of-Entry.pdf
- **Data retention:** Search-derived data can reportedly be stored in CBP's Automated Targeting System (ATS) for **up to 15 years**, per the Pacific Legal Foundation brief. **[SECONDARY — could not verify against a primary CBP/DHS privacy notice directly]**
- **Detention at the airport itself:** Travelers who refuse can be held for extended secondary questioning; U.S. citizens must eventually be let into the country but can still be delayed for hours; non-citizens can be refused entry outright.
- **Fifth Amendment angle (compelled passcode):** Legally unsettled. An attorney quoted by Newsweek frames it as: providing/compelling a *passcode* (as opposed to a biometric like a fingerprint) is arguably "testimonial" and could implicate the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination — but courts have not uniformly resolved this, and CBP's own directive simply treats a locked, uncooperative device as detainable rather than asserting a right to compel the code. **[SECONDARY]** https://www.newsweek.com/cbp-phone-searches-us-citizens-rights-man-charged-device-wiping-12251645

## 5. Is Wiping/Refusing Itself a Crime? — The Tunick Case Charge and Legal Exposure

### The charge actually used: 18 U.S.C. § 2232 — NOT § 1519
The task brief flagged § 1519 as a possibility; **the actual statute charged in this case is § 2232**, "Destruction or removal of property to prevent seizure." This is an important distinction for the piece.

**§ 2232(a) exact language** (Government Publishing Office): "Whoever, before, during, or after any search for or seizure of property by any person authorized to make such search or seizure, knowingly destroys, damages, wastes, disposes of, transfers, or otherwise takes any action... [to prevent the property's seizure]." **Maximum penalty: fine and/or up to 5 years in prison.** **[PRIMARY — U.S. Code text]** https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2021-title18/html/USCODE-2021-title18-partI-chap109-sec2232.htm

**For comparison, 18 U.S.C. § 1519** (the statute the brief anticipated) criminalizes knowingly altering/destroying/concealing "any record, document, or tangible object" with intent to obstruct a federal investigation; it's a Sarbanes-Oxley-era (2002) statute historically used in corporate document-shredding cases (Enron-adjacent). **Maximum penalty: up to 20 years.** § 1519 was **not** the charge brought against Tunick — his max exposure under § 2232 is 5 years, a much shorter statute more narrowly tailored to destroying property specifically to defeat an in-progress seizure. **[SECONDARY — legal explainer]** https://www.thefederalcriminalattorneys.com/destruction-of-records

### Exact indictment language (Tunick case)
"The defendant...did knowingly destroy, damage, waste, dispose of...the digital contents of a Google Pixel cellular phone, for the purpose of preventing and impairing the Government's lawful authority to take said property into its custody." **[SECONDARY, quoting the indictment directly]** https://cybernews.com/privacy/atlanta-man-border-search-prosecuted-grapheneos/

### Legal/expert commentary on this specific case
- **Matthew Dodge**, Tunick's federal public defender: called use of this statute in this context "incredibly rare" — said he found only one other precedent, an unrelated drug-trafficking case. Argued agents "tried to rig this game by making this all come to a head at the airport" rather than getting a warrant. **[SECONDARY]** https://www.404media.co/the-government-hopes-to-set-a-precedent-an-interview-with-the-man-charged-for-allegedly-wiping-his-grapheneos-phone/
- **Runa Sandvik**, security researcher: "authorities may argue you knowingly destroyed data, so it's better to not have that data on you when you cross certain borders." **[SECONDARY]** https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/24/us-accuses-american-of-allegedly-wiping-his-phone-using-a-duress-password-during-border-search/
- **Bill Budington**, EFF technologist: said he had not previously encountered a prosecution like this one. Same TechCrunch article.
- **Christophe Boutry**, cybersecurity expert (quoted via The Guardian, relayed by TechSpot): the prosecution is "concerning – and sends the message that [GrapheneOS] is criminal by default." **[SECONDARY]** https://www.techspot.com/news/113236-us-prosecutors-charge-atlanta-man-after-grapheneos-phone.html
- **EFF (institutional statement, not case-specific)**: "A person's privacy interests in the personal data on a phone or laptop are extraordinarily different from their limited privacy interests in the contents of their suitcase." **[SECONDARY, quoted via CyberNews]** https://cybernews.com/privacy/atlanta-man-border-search-prosecuted-grapheneos/
- General framing across multiple outlets: this is reported as the **first known U.S. federal prosecution** built around a phone's self-wipe/duress-password feature triggered during a border stop.

## 6. This Specific Case — Timeline, Facts, and "Official CBP Statement"

**Case:** *United States v. Samuel Tunick* (federal court, Georgia; Atlanta-area activist connected to the "Defend the Atlanta Forest"/"Cop City" protest movement)

### Timeline
- **June 2024:** GrapheneOS adds a duress-PIN feature that, when entered instead of the real passcode, triggers an immediate, irreversible wipe (deletes encryption keys). **[SECONDARY]** https://decrypt.co/374394/border-agents-phone-duress-passcode-grapheneos
- **January 24, 2025:** Tunick returns to the U.S. via Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport from a trip to the Dominican Republic. Pulled into secondary inspection. Per his defense filings: agents had circulated his name/photo internally citing "suspected terrorism" ties to the Cop City protest movement; he requested a lawyer multiple times and was denied each time; he was not read Miranda rights; officers said they were searching for child exploitation material (his attorney disputes this was the real reason and argues it was pretextual, tied to his activism). No warrant was presented.
- When Tunick entered a passcode, "the screen went blank, flashed several times, and the phone appeared to restart" — the duress PIN wiped the device. Agents seized the phone anyway and allowed him to enter the country.
- **~January 2026 (approx. one year later):** Federal indictment returned, charging Tunick under 18 U.S.C. § 2232.
- **July 23–24, 2026:** First court hearing on a motion to suppress; national media coverage begins (TechCrunch, 404 Media, etc.).
- **Late October 2026 (expected):** Federal judge expected to rule on the suppression motion.
- Tunick has **pleaded not guilty**. Maximum exposure: 5 years.

## 7. Circuit Split on Warrant Requirements for Border Device Searches

Useful context for "whether officers need a warrant" — there is **no warrant requirement nationally**; CBP's own policy requires none, and courts are split on whether the Constitution demands one. As of July 2026:

### Circuits/rulings allowing warrantless/suspicionless manual searches (CBP's position wins)
- **1st Circuit — *Alasaad v. Mayorkas*, 988 F.3d 8 (2021):** Upheld warrantless border searches of devices for contraband/evidence of law violations; no probable cause required. **[SECONDARY synthesis]** https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/smart-phones-at-the-border-what-does-the-fourth-amendment-protect ; case background: https://harvardlawreview.org/print/vol-135/alasaad-v-mayorkas/ ; https://www.clearygottlieb.com/news-and-insights/publication-listing/first-circuit-upholds-border-searches-of-electronic-devices-without-probable-cause
- **7th Circuit — *United States v. Mendez*, 103 F.4th 1303 (2024):** Manual searches are "routine," no suspicion required. **[SECONDARY]** https://www.criminallegalnews.org/news/2024/nov/1/seventh-circuit-announces-search-cellphone-border-constitutes-routine-inspection-and-does-not-require-warrant-probable-cause-or-even-individualized-suspicion/
- **4th Circuit — *United States v. Belmonte Cardozo*, No. 25-4239 (decided July 13, 2026 — one week before this story broke):** Manual phone searches are "routine" border searches requiring no warrant or individualized suspicion; forensic/advanced searches still need reasonable suspicion. **[PRIMARY — EFF's own case writeup]** https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/07/fourth-circuit-says-border-agents-can-search-your-phone-hand-no-suspicion-required — **this is a highly relevant, very recent, directly-on-point primary source for the article's news-peg timing.**
- **11th Circuit — *United States v. Touset*:** No reasonable suspicion or warrant required for forensic searches.
- **8th Circuit — *United States v. Haitao Xiang*:** Follows the 11th Circuit's more permissive approach.

### Circuits/rulings requiring more (reasonable suspicion or a warrant)
- **9th Circuit — *United States v. Cano*, 958 F.3d 1121 (2019/2020):** Manual searches for "digital contraband" need no suspicion, but **forensic ("advanced") searches require reasonable suspicion.** https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2019/08/ninth-circuit-goes-step-further-protect-privacy-border-device-searches ; opinion: https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2020/09/02/17-50151.pdf **[PRIMARY — 9th Cir. opinion PDF]**
- **4th Circuit — *United States v. Kolsuz* (2018)** and ***United States v. Aigbekaen* (2019):** Forensic searches require individualized suspicion of a transnational offense (Kolsuz); a warrant is required if the forensic search is really about a domestic crime (Aigbekaen). Note this sits alongside the *same* circuit's 2026 *Belmonte Cardozo* ruling on manual searches — the 4th Circuit's rule is now split by search *type* (manual vs. forensic), not a blanket rule either way.
- **District court rulings requiring a warrant (not circuit-binding, but influential):**
  - ***United States v. Smith***, S.D.N.Y. (Judge Jed Rakoff, 2023) — first court to hold a warrant is required for a cell phone search at the border absent exigent circumstances. **[SECONDARY]** https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2023/05/federal-judge-makes-history-holding-border-searches-cell-phones-require-warrant ; case page: https://knightcolumbia.org/cases/united-states-v-smith
  - ***United States v. Sultanov***, E.D.N.Y. (ruled July 26, 2024, re: a March 2022 JFK Airport search) — held a warrant based on probable cause is required, though ultimately denied suppression on good-faith grounds; also flagged First Amendment/press-freedom concerns. **[SECONDARY]** https://knightcolumbia.org/content/federal-court-says-warrant-required-for-device-searches-at-the-border
  - ***United States v. Fox*** — also cited alongside Smith/Sultanov as a 2024 S.D.N.Y.-area ruling requiring warrants.
- **Currently pending / unresolved as of 2026:**
  - 2nd Circuit: appeals of Smith/Sultanov-line rulings pending; EFF has filed amicus briefs urging the circuit to require a warrant (as of Nov. 2024). https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2024/11/eff-second-circuit-electronic-device-searches-border-require-warrant
  - 3rd Circuit: *United States v. Roggio* — EFF amicus brief filed February 2026, still pending.
  - Other newer case names surfacing in 2026 EFF material: *Elsharkawi v. United States* (9th Cir.), *United States v. Kamaldoss* (2nd Cir.).
  - https://www.eff.org/issues/border-searches **[PRIMARY]**

**Bottom line for the piece:** There is a live, active circuit split, and it is *not* trending in a single direction — the 4th Circuit's *Belmonte Cardozo* (July 13, 2026) and the 7th Circuit's *Mendez* (2024) both went CBP's way just before/around this story, while scattered district courts in the 2nd Circuit have gone the opposite way and appeals are pending in the 2nd and 3rd Circuits. No warrant is required anywhere today for a *basic/manual* search; the fight is mostly over *advanced/forensic* searches and how far "reasonable suspicion" goes.
