OpenAI Jony Ive Device: $300-400 Speaker Reported for 2027

Bloomberg reports OpenAI's first device is a $300-400 screen-free, doughnut-shaped speaker for 2027. What's confirmed, what's rumor, and the India price math.

OpenAI’s first hardware product is reportedly a screen-free, doughnut-shaped smart speaker about the size of a hockey puck, priced between $300 and $400 and targeting a 2027 launch. That is the core of a Bloomberg report published August 6, echoed within a day by ten-plus outlets. OpenAI has not commented. The reported price is up from February’s $200-300 leak — and the device enters a market where incumbents give AI assistants away.

What the report actually says

Bloomberg’s August 6 story — the most detailed look yet at the Jony Ive-designed device — describes a small, battery-powered speaker meant to be carried from room to room in one hand. It has no display. Instead, per MacRumors’ account of the report, it signals attention with lights that show when it is listening and physical “moving parts that help give it personality,” alongside speaker grilles, microphones, camera systems and environmental sensors. The casing is described as premium metal, and TechCrunch relays the price as “perhaps $300 to $400 per unit.”

Two details matter as much as the specs. First, the timeline: OpenAI “hopes to introduce it later this year ahead of a 2027 launch,” per MacRumors — an unveiling before the product ships, Apple-style. Second, the price has moved. In February, The Information reported a $200-300 target for the same device, with Face ID-style user recognition. Six months later the reported floor is $100 higher. Both numbers are leaks, not announcements; the drift is still the story.

Just as notable is what the report does not say. No manufacturing partner is named — earlier supply-chain reporting first named Luxshare, then Foxconn. No subscription model is described. And the device still has no public name, for reasons that run through two courtrooms.

The vision, at least, is on the record. Sam Altman has said current devices make him feel like he is “walking through Times Square,” and wants something closer to “sitting in the most beautiful cabin by a lake.” Ive told OpenAI’s DevDay audience the team had generated “15 to 20 really compelling product ideas” before narrowing to a first device.

The Apple lawsuit shadow

The report landed in the middle of a legal fight over who owns the craft behind it. On July 10, Apple sued OpenAI, io Products and two former Apple employees in federal court, alleging systematic theft of trade secrets — including the metal-finishing techniques behind Apple’s industrial design, a pointed allegation given the leaked device’s “high-quality metal” construction. Apple’s complaint claims around 400 former Apple employees now work at OpenAI, and alleges some job candidates were asked to bring Apple components and documents to interviews. Ive himself is not a named defendant.

OpenAI’s response has been unusually combative. An August 4 company post titled “Apple is getting this wrong” called the suit “careless, aggressive and oddly personal,” said Apple’s outside counsel initially emailed the wrong person “after confusing two Asian last names,” and quoted hardware chief Tang Tan’s standing instruction that the team “must not use, any confidential information.” On August 6 — the same day the speaker details leaked — OpenAI moved to dismiss the case, per Bloomberg.

The stakes for the device are practical, not just reputational. Apple is seeking a preliminary injunction, and as IP law professor Saurabh Vishnubhakat told Fortune, the risk is that OpenAI must “quarantine files or employees, redo engineering work, abandon a manufacturing process, or replace a supplier” — any of which could stretch that 2027 date.

A $300-400 bet against free assistants

The competitive math is brutal. Amazon made Alexa+, its generative-AI assistant, free for all US Prime subscribers in February ($19.99 a month otherwise) and is now live in ten countries. Google launched a $99.99 Home Speaker in June with Gemini built in. Apple’s HomePod line has idled — the mini is largely unchanged since 2020 while Apple’s Siri overhaul slips — but even an idle incumbent anchors prices. Against a $99.99 Google speaker and a free Alexa upgrade, OpenAI would ask three to four times as much.

Recent history is unforgiving here. Humane’s AI Pin launched at $699 and was dead within a year — servers off February 28, 2025, assets sold to HP for $116 million. Rabbit’s $199 R1 survived a scathing launch only as a novelty. The Friend pendant relaunched this summer at $249 plus a $9.99 monthly fee into protests over always-on listening. The one clear winner sold AI inside an object people already wanted: Meta and EssilorLuxottica moved over 7 million smart glasses in 2025. A speaker at least passes that test — it is useful even when the AI is not — though the pattern in our Chips & Hardware coverage is that hardware pricing forgives very little.

OpenAI’s reasons for trying anyway are also on the record. ChatGPT reached 900 million weekly users and 50 million paying consumer subscribers in February, and annualized revenue passed $20 billion in 2025, per CFO Sarah Friar — scale that funds hardware experiments. But distribution is the deeper motive: on phones, Apple and Google now ship rival assistants as OS defaults, the same gatekeeper squeeze that shaped OpenAI’s aggressive API price cuts. As analyst Anshel Sag put it to Fortune, “They’re being forced to build hardware because it gives them more control over the user experience.” A device OpenAI owns end to end is an exit from other companies’ home screens — if people buy it, and if the compute economics behind every AI product leave room for hardware margins.

The India angle

The gap in every writeup so far, including the Indian ones: none of the Bloomberg-derived coverage — Business Standard, Outlook Business, Digit.in included — says a word about India. That silence is striking, because India is OpenAI’s second-largest market, with over 100 million weekly ChatGPT users, and OpenAI has spent a year courting it: a free year of ChatGPT Go for every Indian user, new offices, and Tata data-center capacity.

What would $300-400 mean here? The closest precedent is Apple’s HomePod, a $299 speaker that launched in India at ₹32,900 — roughly a third above straight currency conversion once duties, GST and channel margins stack up. Amazon’s and Apple’s smart speakers have consistently carried a 30-38% India markup at launch. Apply that pattern to the reported $300-400 range and you get roughly ₹33,000 to ₹47,000 — our extrapolation from those precedents, not a reported figure. For context, Amazon’s newest Echo Dot Max sells in India for ₹10,999 and the Echo Studio for ₹23,999, and Alexa+ itself has not reached India — Amazon’s own India launch page says the new Echos will support it “once available in India.”

India was Amazon’s first Asian market for Echo back in 2017, and Apple’s recent HomePods reached India within days of the US. If OpenAI’s device ships in 2027 and India is not in the first wave, that will say something about how the company weighs its second-largest market against its richest one.

What to watch

Four things will tell us whether this stays a 2027 product. Whether the teased 2026 unveiling actually happens — the project has already slipped once, from a late-2026 target, over what FT-sourced reporting described as personality, privacy and compute problems. How the court rules on Apple’s injunction request and OpenAI’s motion to dismiss, either of which could reshape suppliers and schedules. Who finally assembles the thing, after the Luxshare-then-Foxconn flip-flop — component costs are already squeezing device makers, as we saw with Qualcomm’s chip price hike. And whether a subscription appears: no report yet says how a $300-400 speaker relates to ChatGPT’s paid tiers, which is the difference between a gadget and a Trojan horse.

The unresolved tension is the camera-and-microphone question. A device designed to sit in your home, watch its surroundings and learn your patterns arrives into a public that remembers the FTC’s $25 million settlement over Alexa’s children’s recordings and is currently protesting an always-listening pendant. Ive says the goal is a device that makes people feel calm. The bar it must clear is making them feel safe.

Frequently asked questions

How much will the OpenAI device cost?

There is no official price. Bloomberg's August 6 report puts it in the 300-to-400-dollar range. The Information reported a 200-to-300-dollar target in February 2026, so the reported figure has crept upward as the project matures. OpenAI has not commented on either number.

When will the OpenAI Jony Ive device launch?

Reports consistently say 2027, and Bloomberg says OpenAI hopes to show the device before the end of 2026. The timeline has already slipped once — Altman's team originally aimed for late 2026 — so treat every date as a target, not a promise.

What is io, and why was the name dropped?

io Products was the hardware startup Jony Ive co-founded, which OpenAI absorbed in a reported 6.5-billion-dollar all-stock deal that closed in July 2025. A trademark suit by hearing-device startup iyO led courts to bar the 'io' name for AI devices; OpenAI dropped it, and the case settled confidentially on July 28, 2026.

Will the OpenAI speaker be available in India?

No source mentions India availability yet. India is OpenAI's second-largest market with 100-million-plus weekly ChatGPT users, which argues for an early launch. If OpenAI follows the 30-to-38-percent import markup pattern of past US smart speakers, the device would land roughly between 33,000 and 47,000 rupees — our estimate, not a reported figure.
