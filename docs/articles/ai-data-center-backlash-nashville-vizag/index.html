AI Data Center Backlash: Nashville to Vizag, Cities Push Back

Nashville voted 27-5 to take a $700M data center site by eminent domain, and India's courts test Google's $15B Vizag hub. Inside the local pushback on AI's buildout.

The local fight over AI’s physical footprint went global this week. On Tuesday evening, Nashville’s Metro Council voted 27-5 to let the city seize the site of a planned $700 million data center next to its zoo — using eminent domain. The same week, India’s Andhra Pradesh High Court directed the state to answer a public-interest petition against Google’s $15 billion AI hub in Visakhapatnam, with a hearing set for August 24. Cities and courts, not chip supplies, are becoming the friction in AI’s buildout.

What Nashville actually voted for

The bill, BL2026-1489, passed its third and final reading on August 4. It authorizes Metro Nashville to acquire a roughly 23.5-acre parcel at 648 Grassmere Park — bought in July for $23 million by Atlanta-based developer DC BLOX — by negotiation or condemnation. The claimed public use is not the zoo: it is relocated city offices, warehouse and training space, needed because an FAA-approved runway expansion at Nashville’s airport will displace an existing Metro campus — and office space, the city argues, satisfies the condemnation law’s public-use test.

The price of stopping the project may be steep. Metro’s own appraisal of the land is $37.4 million, the likely floor for “just compensation” — well above what DC BLOX paid weeks earlier. Not everyone was comfortable: council member Ginny Welsch called the move a “gross misuse” of condemnation power.

The emotion is real. Nashville Zoo says the facility — pitched at 10 megawatts initially, with the zoo warning of far higher figures — would put constant cooling noise and light pollution beside 3,000 animals, including a clouded-leopard breeding programme and the federally protected Nashville crayfish. A petition against the project has passed 500,000 signatures. DC BLOX, which says its design addresses the noise concerns, said only that it had “no updates to share” after the vote; its attorney has said condemning land to interfere with a private purchase raises “serious legal questions” under Tennessee law. Such cases typically take 12 to 18 months, so the blocking move starts a process rather than ending one. It also capped a deliberate three-step campaign: two weeks earlier the council unanimously passed a data-center zoning code and a temporary permit moratorium — define, pause, then take.

What makes the mechanism striking is the inversion. Across the US, eminent domain has lately worked for data centers: Georgia Power is acquiring about 30 residential properties to expand grid capacity largely serving data centers. Nashville just pointed the same tool the other way.

The India angle

India’s version runs through courtrooms, and its stakes are water. The Andhra Pradesh High Court this week directed the state government to respond to a petition from Jal Biradari, a water-conservation group, against the $15 billion Google–AdaniConneX–Airtel AI hub in Visakhapatnam, with the next hearing on August 24. The petitioners argue the gigawatt-scale campus would strain a city that already receives about 410 million litres of water a day against a 480-million-litre requirement, and that one campus sits roughly 860 metres from the Kambalakonda Wildlife Sanctuary. It is one of several challenges the project has drawn this year: the Human Rights Forum has separately filed three cases at India’s environmental court over water drawn from a rural drinking-water scheme.

Google, which broke ground in April on the nearly one-gigawatt project, says it will use “advanced air cooling to protect vital local water resources.” The state calls the objections misleading; it has courted the project with land discounts, power-tariff rebates and GST reimbursements that a rival state minister publicly questioned. And the buildout is not waiting: on August 6, Microsoft’s fourth Indian cloud region went live in Hyderabad, advertising zero-water cooling, on the back of a cumulative $20.5 billion India commitment. India crossed 1,700 MW of data-centre capacity at the end of 2025, per CBRE, and is projected to add about 30% more in 2026 — while researchers project the sector’s water use more than doubling by 2030 in one of the world’s most water-stressed large economies. The tension Nashville expresses through zoning, India expresses through water.

The direction is not uniform — county boards in Arkansas, North Carolina and Texas all rejected proposed moratoriums this year. But the trend line is unmistakable.

Why resistance is spiking now

Data Center Watch, the tracker most often cited, counted $64 billion in US projects blocked or delayed between May 2024 and March 2025 — then roughly $130 billion in the first quarter of 2026 alone, across more than 75 projects, with opposition groups active in 49 states.

Three drivers explain it. First, scale: the IEA projects global data-centre electricity use roughly doubling to about 950 TWh by 2030, with AI-focused facilities tripling their consumption, and in the US it expects data centers to account for about half of all electricity-demand growth to 2030. Second, bills: PJM’s independent market monitor tied $23 billion in customer price increases through 2028 primarily to data-center demand in America’s largest grid region; IEEFA estimates capacity-market effects alone add $16-21 a month to some household bills. Third, the money is unprecedented and visible: the four biggest hyperscalers guided to a combined ~$725 billion of 2026 capex, up 77% in a year — the same buildout we traced in the real cost of running AI.

Public opinion has followed the bills. Gallup found 71% of Americans oppose an AI data center in their own area. Pew’s read is more precise about why: 39% say data centers are mostly bad for the environment and 38% for energy costs, against just 15% for local jobs — people believe the economic upside but doubt who pays the physical costs.

What it means

The industry’s answer is jobs and tax revenue; the honest reading: real, but smaller than advertised. An industry-commissioned PwC study claims data centers support 5.5 million US jobs; Brookings’ independent county-level analysis finds hyperscale facilities do lift local employment 4-5% over five to six years — thousands of jobs in a typical county, not tens of thousands — and concludes industry estimates overstate the effect by about a factor of three.

Data centers have also become electoral: Virginia’s 2025 governor’s race, in the state with the world’s largest data-center concentration, put the issue on the debate stage. Washington pulls the other way: a July 2025 executive order fast-tracks federal permitting of large AI facilities, and the president attacked New York’s permit pause within a day, calling data centers “LIQUID GOLD.” For AI companies, siting risk now belongs on the same planning sheet as chips and power-purchase agreements: the constraint on the price war among frontier models may ultimately be a county commission, a high court, or a zoo. For communities, Nashville and Vizag are templates — one fiscal, one judicial — and unlike Europe’s rules-first approach, the US and India are writing their AI-infrastructure law one flashpoint at a time.

What to watch

Whether DC BLOX sues: no independent legal scholar has yet publicly assessed whether Nashville’s “public use” would survive a pretextual-taking challenge. The August 24 hearing in Andhra Pradesh, and whether Vizag’s construction pace changes before it. New York’s statewide environmental study, which will define what a “moratorium with an off-ramp” looks like. And the money: if hyperscaler capex keeps compounding while the blocked-project tally grows in step, expect the industry to shift from fighting sites one by one to pushing state-level permitting reform — the quiet battle we track across our Policy & Society coverage.

Frequently asked questions

Do data centers raise electricity bills?

In the biggest US grid region, PJM, the independent market monitor has tied billions of dollars in customer price increases through 2028 primarily to data-center demand, and household estimates there run to roughly twenty dollars a month. But attribution is contested — some economists argue transmission costs and policy choices matter as much, so the honest answer is: it depends on your grid, and the pressure is newest and clearest where AI construction is densest.

Why did Nashville block the data center near its zoo?

The zoo argued round-the-clock noise and light from the facility would harm its animals, including a sensitive clouded-leopard breeding programme, and residents worried about grid strain. The council's vote authorizes the city to acquire the site — by eminent domain if needed — for public uses like relocated city offices, so the block is not final until the acquisition closes.

How much water does a data center use?

It varies enormously with cooling design and climate. A large facility can use millions of gallons a day, which is why operators now advertise air-cooled and zero-water designs. In India, researchers project national data-centre water use will more than double by 2030, which is what the Vizag petitioners are litigating about.

Will living near a data center lower my property value?

The evidence is mixed. A Brookings study of two decades of US facilities found no significant effect on home prices, while realtors report buyer hesitation near loud or highly visible sites. Noise lawsuits in Michigan and Wisconsin suggest the experience depends heavily on how close you are and how the facility is cooled.
