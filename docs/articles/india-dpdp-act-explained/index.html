India's DPDP Act: What Actually Changes for You, and When

India's DPDP Act explained with exact dates: which privacy rights are live in July 2026, which arrive in 2027, penalties, and what you can do today.
By Saurab Jain · Published July 26, 2026 ·5 min read

India’s Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act will eventually give every Indian app user rights to notice, consent, correction and erasure — but the single most misunderstood fact about it, as of July 2026, is that almost none of those rights are legally binding yet. The Act received assent in August 2023, the Rules were notified on 13 November 2025, and the obligations users will actually feel arrive in two waves: consent managers on 13 November 2026, and the full set of user-facing rights, children’s-data protections and penalties on 13 May 2027 (DPDP Rules, 2025 — Gazette G.S.R. 846(E)).

The timeline that most coverage skips

The DPDP Act, 2023 sat dormant for over two years after Parliament passed it in August 2023, because a law of this kind only bites once its subordinate rules exist (DPDP Act text, MeitY). The Rules landed on 13 November 2025 with a deliberately phased schedule (Bar & Bench). What went live immediately was machinery: definitions, the legal existence of the Data Protection Board, and an amendment narrowing the RTI Act’s personal-information exemption. Twelve months later — 13 November 2026 — registration opens for consent managers. Everything a user would actually notice — plain-language consent notices, the right to erasure, breach notifications, verifiable parental consent for under-18s, and the penalty schedule — commences 13 May 2027.

That means a paradoxical reading of 2026: companies are racing to comply, but if an app mishandles your data today, DPDP itself offers you no enforceable remedy yet. Any 2025–2026 headline about an Indian “data fine” is almost certainly action under a different law — the IT Act’s Section 43A, CERT-In’s directions, or the IT Rules — and one independent tracker logs exactly zero DPDP enforcement actions through June 2026 (Kensara enforcement tracker).

What changes when the rights arrive

From May 2027, consent requests must be standalone, itemised and written in plain language, with withdrawal as easy as agreement, and each user gets the option to read the notice in English or any of the 22 languages in the Constitution’s Eighth Schedule — the user’s choice per notice, not a mandate that every app publish all 22 translations up front, a nuance many summaries get wrong (Cyril Amarchand Mangaldas FAQ).

Users also gain rights to access, correct and erase their data, a 90-day outer window for responses, and a distinctive nominee right — you can name someone to exercise your data rights if you die or lose capacity, a provision with no direct GDPR equivalent. Children’s data gets India’s strictest treatment: “child” means anyone under 18, and consent must be verifiably parental — a reliable identity check such as a DigiLocker-based token, not a checkbox — with narrow exemptions for schools, healthcare and child-transport safety, none of which permit ads or profiling.

Penalties are large but often misquoted: ₹250 crore per instance for failing on security safeguards, ₹200 crore for not notifying a breach or for children’s-data violations, ₹150 crore for a significant data fiduciary breaching its extra duties — and, unusually, up to ₹10,000 on an individual user who files knowingly false complaints (Cyril Amarchand Mangaldas FAQ). The ₹500-crore ceiling still cited by some compliance vendors comes from the withdrawn 2022 draft bill, not the enacted Schedule.

What the Act deliberately does not do

Three absences define DPDP as much as its rights. First, there is no data-localization mandate: cross-border transfers are permitted by default, subject to a government blacklist that, as of mid-2026, names no countries at all. Second, there is no special category for sensitive data — health records and food-delivery history sit in the same legal tier, where GDPR would treat them very differently. Third, government agencies enjoy broad exemptions for sovereignty and public order, the feature most under fire in court.

That court fight is live: consolidated petitions before the Supreme Court challenge the government exemptions, the Board’s independence, and the RTI amendment, with notice issued on 16 February 2026 and hearings continuing through the spring — the bench has framed “what is public data and what is personal data” as a central question, and no ruling had issued as of late July 2026 (LiveLaw).

The Board itself is the other open question. It exists on paper as a digital-first tribunal, but MeitY was still advertising for its chairperson and four members on 6 May 2026 (MeitY recruitment notice), and public reporting on whether appointments have since been made is conflicting. Until the Board is visibly staffed and the May 2027 provisions commence, there is no DPDP complaints counter to walk up to.

What to watch: the AI question and the court

DPDP excludes personal data that the individual has made publicly available — which sounds like a wide-open door for training AI models on scraped public posts. Yet MeitY told the Rajya Sabha in 2024 that scraping publicly available personal data still attracts the Act’s obligations, a position in visible tension with the statute’s text, and its November 2025 AI guidance is explicitly non-binding. Whether India’s copyright and data-protection regimes permit AI training on public content is also being tested from the copyright side, as we covered in the ANI v. OpenAI fair-dealing ruling. For now the honest answer is: unsettled, with the Supreme Court’s public-versus-personal-data reasoning likely to shape it.

What you can actually do today

Until May 2027, your working toolkit is pre-DPDP. Every significant platform must publish a grievance officer under the IT Rules 2021, with a 24-hour acknowledgment and 15-day resolution clock. CERT-In’s 2022 directions already require companies to report breaches within six hours — much stricter than DPDP’s forthcoming 72-hour user notification. Consumer courts take deficient-service claims about data mishandling, and High Courts have entertained writ petitions in the Board’s absence (Future of Privacy Forum overview). And the settings that matter most are the ones you control now: check what a chatbot retains about you — our guide to what AI chatbots know about you walks through each provider — and prefer services that treat training as opt-in rather than opt-out.

The DPDP Act is real, its dates are now fixed, and its arrival will be the biggest change in Indian consumer privacy law ever. Frequently asked questions

Is the DPDP Act actually in force right now?

Only partially. The Act was passed in August 2023 and the Rules were notified on 13 November 2025, but as of July 2026 only the machinery provisions — definitions and the Data Protection Board's setup — are live. Consent notices, erasure rights, children's-data rules and penalties all commence on 13 May 2027.

Can I force an app to delete my data today under DPDP?

Not yet as a DPDP right — erasure becomes binding on 13 May 2027. Until then, use the app's grievance officer under the IT Rules 2021, which requires acknowledgment within 24 hours and resolution within 15 days, or consumer-court remedies.

Does the DPDP Act force companies to keep Indian data in India?

No. Transfers are allowed to any country by default; the government may notify a blacklist of restricted destinations, and as of mid-2026 that list is empty. This is the opposite of GDPR's restrictive-by-default approach to transfers.

How big are DPDP fines, really?

The Schedule's ceilings run from fifty crore to two hundred and fifty crore rupees per instance depending on the violation — security-safeguard failures at the top, breach non-notification and children's-data violations next, then significant-fiduciary failures. The five-hundred-crore figure that circulates online comes from a withdrawn 2022 draft, not the enacted law.
