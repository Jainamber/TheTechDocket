On November 11, 2026, indie developer Bo Lau launched *Term Sheet, Please*, a browser-based parody game that adapts the dystopian document-checking mechanics of *Papers, Please* into a high-stakes simulation of Silicon Valley venture capital. Players step into the shoes of a venture capitalist reviewing seed-stage startup applications immediately following a Y Combinator Demo Day.

The game arrives at a moment of intense scrutiny for both early-stage startup metrics and the venture capital model itself. By turning the serious, often opaque process of startup due diligence into a gamified puzzle, the project highlights the shifting nature of how tech ideas are validated. For developers and founders navigating the modern ecosystem of [AI tools](/topics/ai-tools/), the game is a humorous yet sharp critique of the industry's funding mechanics.

## Inside the Gameplay of Term Sheet, Please

*Term Sheet, Please* is a free, browser-based point-and-click puzzle game that tasks players with analyzing founder pitches, financial records, and operational metrics to make funding decisions. Operating on a client-side JavaScript engine, the game currently features a single scenario focused on seed-stage founder evaluation.

The gameplay loop translates the bureaucratic tension of border control into the high-pressure environment of a venture capital desk. Players must carefully inspect a founder's physical file, which contains a pitch deck, capitalization tables, financial statements, and historical performance metrics. By clicking on specific data points, players can cross-reference claims to identify discrepancies. For instance, if a founder claims a certain monthly recurring revenue in their pitch deck but their bank statements show a different figure, the player must flag the inconsistency.

Once the documents are analyzed, players can call the founder to their desk by clicking the "NEXT" button. This initiates a phone-based interrogation where the player can question the founder about the flagged errors. After the interview, players can send follow-up mail notes to verify specific metrics, a mechanic that triggers at 17:00 in-game time. The final step requires the player to physically place a decision sheet on the desk, logging their choice to either "PASS", "FUND", or "HOLD" on the investment.

The game is free to play, does not specify an open-source license, and currently showcases a single playable case with simulated founder metrics. It runs directly in the browser using client-side JavaScript without requiring any external downloads. While the game currently limits players to this single scenario, its creator has captured the attention of the developer community by highlighting how easily early-stage metrics can be misaligned.

## The Attribution Debate and Player Feedback

The release of *Term Sheet, Please* sparked immediate controversy on Hacker News, accumulating over 210 points and 80 comments within its first day of launch, according to discussions on [Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50457230). The primary debate centered on the game's lack of explicit attribution to Lucas Pope, the original creator of *Papers, Please*.

Shortly after the game went live, users on the community thread pointed out that the website did not credit the original 2013 indie classic that inspired its entire visual style and mechanical framework. The game garnered 213 points and 85 comments on [Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50457230) within 12 hours, with several prominent commenters urging the creator to add a clear credit line linking back to the original game's homepage. Some users defended the project, arguing that parody and satire are legally protected forms of expression that do not strictly require licensing. However, the consensus among developers was that proper attribution remains an essential ethical standard in the indie software community.

Beyond the copyright debate, players offered significant technical and user interface feedback on the [Hacker News discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50457230). Many noted that the game lacks an "acclimatization ramp," introducing too many complex financial documents simultaneously without a proper tutorial. Others pointed out that the document management area is too cramped, making it difficult to drag multi-page PDFs across the virtual desktop. Text truncation in the dialogue boxes also presented usability issues, with founder responses occasionally cutting off mid-sentence.

This mechanical friction mirrors the real-world complexity of analyzing early-stage startups, where investors are inundated with messy, unstructured data. In an era where venture capital is grappling with shifting market cycles—such as the dynamics discussed in our analysis of how [OpenAI slows down AI training](/articles/openai-slows-ai-training/)—the game serves as a timely reminder of the necessity of rigorous due diligence.

## The India angle

Real-world venture capital in India has experienced a sharp contraction, with the local presence in Y Combinator cohorts dropping from 64 Indian startups in 2021 down to just 1 in 2025. This dramatic shift, discussed widely on [Reddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/StartUpIndia/comments/1pqsnxy/why_has_the_number_of_indian_startups_in_y/), reflects a broader pivot toward global software-as-a-service and artificial intelligence.

Despite the shrinking cohort sizes, YC's historical bets in India have yielded massive financial returns. The accelerator has backed major domestic unicorns, including the payment gateway Razorpay, which joined in the Winter 2015 batch and reached a valuation of $7.5 billion, according to official records from [Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/razorpay).

These early investments translated into significant liquidity events. In late 2025, YC secured nearly $200 million in cash through partial stake sales during the blockbuster public listings of its portfolio companies, as reported by [Asia Tech Review](https://www.asiatechreview.com/p/y-combinator-reaps-big-rewards-in). Specifically, the public debut of the investment platform Groww in November 2025 netted YC approximately $117 million while leaving it with a 10% remaining stake worth roughly $1 billion, while the social commerce platform Meesho's transaction in December 2025 netted the accelerator about $10 million with $50 million in remaining stock, according to [Asia Tech Review](https://www.asiatechreview.com/p/y-combinator-reaps-big-rewards-in).

For local developers attempting to build and host interactive applications similar to the game discussed on [Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50457230), the financial math is heavily influenced by Indian regulatory and banking fees. If an Indian engineer builds an AI-driven web application using US-based cloud infrastructure with a base cost of $100, standard bank forex card markups of 3.5% add $3.50, while India's Integrated Goods and Services Tax (IGST) adds an 18% levy of $18.63 on the transaction, bringing the total landed cost to $122.13—a substantial 22.13% markup on raw dollar pricing. These transaction frictions make cost-efficiency critical, a reality that developers also face when assessing rising infrastructure expenses, such as those detailed in our breakdown of the [DeepSeek API price hike](/articles/deepseek-api-price-hike/).

## Evaluating Venture Capital Portfolios

Comparing fictional due diligence with real-world investment outcomes reveals how early-stage accelerators balance high-risk seed rounds against massive public market exits. While the game simplifies the process into a single-desk decision, actual venture capital involves complex regulatory compliance and long-term liquidity planning.

To understand how the simplified mechanics of *Term Sheet, Please* compare to the operational realities of actual YC-backed giants in the Indian market, we can examine the core metrics that drive funding decisions.

| Attribute | *Term Sheet, Please* (Game) | Razorpay (W15 Batch) | Groww (2025 IPO) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Setting / Focus** | Seed round evaluation post-Demo Day | Payment gateway & financial SaaS | Investment & wealth platform |
| **Core Decision Metric** | Pitch deck facts & founder consistency | Transaction volume & merchant growth | Active investor accounts & AUM |
| **Reported Financial Delta** | Not applicable (fictional seed stage) | Valued at $7.5 billion ([Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/razorpay)) | Netted YC $117 million in partial sale ([Asia Tech Review](https://www.asiatechreview.com/p/y-combinator-reaps-big-rewards-in)) |
| **Due Diligence Focus** | Spotting document and financial errors | Regulatory compliance & RBI licenses | KYC compliance & market regulations |

In the game, a single document mismatch is enough to warrant a "PASS" or "HOLD." In reality, early-stage investors often overlook minor administrative discrepancies if the core growth metrics—such as transaction volume or customer acquisition costs—show exponential potential. However, as startups mature toward public listings, regulatory compliance becomes the ultimate gatekeeper.
